# Findings

What the pipeline in this repository measured, and what came out of it. Every number
below is regenerated by `./run_all.sh` from the CSVs in `data/` — nothing here is
transcribed by hand.

## The question

Does adopting an AI coding assistant (Cursor, GitHub Copilot) change the
source-detectable accessibility of a React/TypeScript codebase?

"Source-detectable" is the load-bearing qualifier. The analyzer reads component
source: semantic HTML structure, keyboard affordances, ARIA usage, heading and
landmark structure. It does not and cannot see runtime properties — computed colour
contrast, live focus order, dynamically toggled `aria-hidden`. Those are out of scope
by construction, not by oversight.

## The corpus

| | |
|---|---|
| Repositories | 446 open-source React/TypeScript projects |
| Adopters / non-adopters | 189 / 257 |
| Repo-months | 13,702 |
| Primary panel | 181 repos (95 adopter, 86 non-adopter), propensity-matched |
| Measured axes | 4 (a 5th was near-constant and dropped) |

The 181-repo matched panel is the primary comparison; the full 446-repo panel is the
robustness check. Matching corrects the obvious selection problem — adopters skew
toward larger, busier projects. Contributor count starts at a standardised mean
difference of 1.10 between the arms and lands at 0.04 after matching; every covariate
finishes under 0.16.

## Headline: no detectable effect, and a tight bound on how small it could be

Adoption shows no measurable change in accessibility on any axis. Not "we couldn't
find one" — the estimates are small, consistently slightly negative (marginally
*fewer* issues, never significantly), and stable across two independent estimators on
both panels.

**Matched panel (181 repos).** ATT is the average treatment effect in native units;
`% base` expresses it against the pre-adoption adopter mean.

| Axis | ATT | SE | p | wild-cluster p | % base |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Semantic HTML | −0.0043 | 0.0040 | 0.285 | 0.357 | −0.5% |
| Keyboard/focus | −0.0016 | 0.0037 | 0.658 | 0.688 | −0.2% |
| WCAG total density | −0.0016 | 0.0023 | 0.493 | 0.520 | −4.6% |
| WCAG operable density | −0.0016 | 0.0019 | 0.414 | 0.435 | −8.6% |
| Severity-weighted | −0.0029 | 0.0048 | 0.552 | 0.565 | −3.6% |

**Full panel (446 repos)** reproduces it: every p between 0.41 and 0.82, every point
estimate smaller still.

The more useful statement is the equivalence bound — not "no effect found" but "an
effect this large is ruled out":

| Axis | Matched panel | Full panel |
|---|---:|---:|
| Semantic HTML | ±5% | ±5% |
| Keyboard/focus | ±5% | ±5% |
| WCAG total density | ±20% | ±15% |
| WCAG operable density | ±30% | ±20% |
| Severity-weighted | ±15% | ±10% |

On the two dense score axes — where most components carry signal — anything larger
than ±5% of baseline is statistically excluded. The violation-density axes certify
only at ±10–30%, and the reason is arithmetic rather than weak data: those axes are
rare-event counts with baselines near zero (WCAG-operable density ≈ 0.018; most
components have no violations at all). Their absolute confidence half-widths are
*smaller* than the score axes'; dividing by a near-zero baseline is what inflates the
percentage.

## The checks that could have broken it

Each of these was run because it had a real chance of overturning the result.

**Parallel trends.** Joint F-test on pre-adoption event-study coefficients at
k = −6, −3, −2 on the semantic-HTML axis: F = 0.46 (3 df, p ≈ 0.71). No evidence
against the identifying assumption.

**Accumulation over time.** Full event study over k ∈ [−12, +12] shows no debt piling
up post-adoption. Coefficients sit near zero throughout, drifting to about −0.010 at
twelve months — the *fewer-issues* direction — with no bin individually significant.

**Multiple comparisons.** Benjamini–Hochberg across the five tested axis p-values:
every adjusted q = 0.658. Splitting by WCAG principle finds nothing either —
perceivable ATT = +0.0002 (p = 0.781), robust ATT = −0.0002 (p = 0.769).
*Understandable* is identically zero across the corpus and is reported as such rather
than fitted as a degenerate model, as is the ARIA-correctness axis (mean 0.9997,
near-zero variance).

**Specification sensitivity.** Shifting the adoption date ±1 month, dropping repos
with under 18 months of history, and restricting adopters to those with hard
configuration-artifact evidence all return the same null. No p below 0.16. The
hard-config subset is small (n = 10) so it is directionally consistent rather than
confirmatory.

**Ceiling censoring — the one that mattered most.** Both score axes pile up at their
upper bound: 24.2% of matched repo-months sit at exactly 1.0 on semantic HTML, 42.8%
on keyboard/focus. Under a ceiling, a genuinely negative effect is attenuated toward
zero — precisely the direction that would fabricate a false null. Re-estimating with
an upper-censored Tobit (Chamberlain–Mundlak correlated random effects, since a Tobit
carrying repo dummies is inconsistent at fixed T):

| Axis | At ceiling | Linear ATT | Tobit latent β | Tobit APE | APE % base |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Semantic HTML | 24.2% | −0.0043 (p=0.285) | −0.0099 (p=0.271) | −0.0065 (p=0.266) | −0.68% |
| Keyboard/focus | 42.8% | −0.0016 (p=0.658) | −0.0053 (p=0.571) | −0.0026 (p=0.567) | −0.27% |

The latent coefficient is roughly twice the linear ATT — exactly the attenuation the
ceiling predicts, and exactly what the estimator's self-test on simulated censored
panels reproduces (`python analysis/tobit_enriched.py --selftest`). The correction
moves the estimates in the expected direction by the expected amount and leaves both
well inside the ±5% bound. The largest of them is 1.0% of baseline.

## The one thing that did move

Looking past the mean — at volatility, upper-tail risk, and how long a repository
stays in a given quality state — the *level* of every moment is unchanged, with one
robust exception. Post-adoption quality states are modestly stickier: the spectral gap
of the fitted Markov chain drops by 0.019 (95% CI [−0.031, −0.007]), a slower-mixing
chain.

Read plainly: AI-assisted repositories show less month-to-month churn in their
accessibility profile, without that profile getting better or worse. Less movement,
not better or worse code.

## Two results that were wrong before they were right

Both are worth more than the substantive null, because both are mistakes any analyst
would make on a panel shaped like this one.

**A pooled tail test that read p = 0.003 and was noise.** Testing upper-tail
exceedance by pooling pre- against post-period observations produced an apparently
significant harmful effect. It reversed to p = 0.933 once the test contrasted adopters
against non-adopters and clustered within repository. A Monte Carlo over synthetic
panels *with no effect injected* quantifies the failure: in the presence of a common
secular trend, the pooled test rejects a true null up to 100% of the time, while the
cluster-robust difference-in-differences holds its nominal 5% rate
(`analysis/size_distortion_sim.py`).

Both arms were drifting together. Pooling reads shared drift as treatment effect; the
adopter-vs-non-adopter contrast nets it out.

**A degrees-of-freedom degeneracy in Markov regime tests.** Quantile-binning a
zero-inflated outcome collapses the effective degrees of freedom of a Markov
homogeneity test, silently invalidating it. Demonstrated in
`analysis/result2_simulation.py`.

And one conjecture retracted rather than defended: snapshot-cadence birth–death
utilization was assumed biased in level but unbiased in the pre/post contrast. A
simulation written to confirm that cancellation refuted it instead
(`analysis/utilization_refutation_sim.py`), so the queueing apparatus was dropped from
the analysis entirely.

The general lesson, which is the transferable part: on repository panels, always
contrast treated against control rather than before against after, and always cluster
within repository. Zero-inflated outcomes will manufacture a hypothesis-confirming
result if you don't.

## Is the measurement any good?

The semantic-HTML score was validated against four React/TypeScript engineers scoring
53 stratified components by hand. Krippendorff's α = 0.870 (95% CI [0.776, 0.923]),
and α = 0.880 (95% CI [0.787, 0.934]) recomputed on the three raters who did not
design the metric. Per-rater Spearman ρ against the automated score runs 0.670–0.751
(pooled 0.733). See `analysis/raters_3independent.py`.

Roughly 15% of the validation sample was genuinely ambiguous, mostly components built
on headless UI libraries (Radix, Ariakit, Chakra) that wrap semantic elements the
static analyzer cannot follow through.

## Why static analysis at all

A headless-browser pipeline failed to render 60.4% of the component snapshots in this
corpus. Render-independent AST analysis buys 100% coverage at the cost of
runtime-only checks. For a longitudinal panel — where a component that fails to mount
is a missing observation, not a neutral one — that trade is the difference between a
measurable panel and an unmeasurable one.

## Limits worth stating

- Monthly snapshot cadence averages away introduce-and-fix events that happen within a
  month. Finer temporal resolution, not more repositories, is the lever that would
  tighten the sparse axes.
- Equivalence is certified at ±5% on the dense score axes but only ±10–30% on the
  sparse density axes.
- Open-source React/TypeScript repositories only. Nothing here extends to private
  codebases, other frameworks, or other quality attributes.
- Adoption is inferred from configuration artifacts and commit signals, not from
  observing developers. The hard-signal subset is small.

## Reading this alongside qualm

[qualm](https://github.com/SomilKSharma/qualm) is the developer-facing linter built on
the same render-independent AST approach. It inherits the measurement technique from
this pipeline and deliberately does *not* inherit any causal claim: a qualm finding
says *this code has an accessibility defect worth fixing*, never *an AI tool caused
it*. There is no measured adoption effect here to attribute anything to, which is
exactly why qualm weights its categories by WCAG user impact rather than by estimated
effect size.
